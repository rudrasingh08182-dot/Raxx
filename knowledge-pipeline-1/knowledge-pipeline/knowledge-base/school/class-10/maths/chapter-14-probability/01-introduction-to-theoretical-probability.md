The theory of probability is a fundamental branch of mathematics with significant practical importance. In this chapter, we focus on the theoretical (or classical) approach to probability. 

When we perform an experiment, such as tossing a 'fair' coin or throwing a 'fair' die, we assume the outcomes are 'equally likely'. This means there is no bias or interference, and each outcome has the same possibility of occurring. For example, when tossing a coin, the outcomes 'head' and 'tail' are equally likely. Similarly, for a die, the outcomes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 are equally likely. 

However, not all experiments have equally likely outcomes. For instance, in a bag with 4 red balls and 1 blue ball, drawing a red ball is more likely than drawing a blue ball. From now on, we will assume that all experiments discussed in this chapter have equally likely outcomes.
