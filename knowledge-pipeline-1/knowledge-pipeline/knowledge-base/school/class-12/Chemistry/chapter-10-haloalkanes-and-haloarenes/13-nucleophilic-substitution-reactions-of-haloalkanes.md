A reaction in which an already existing nucleophile in a molecule is replaced by another is called a nucleophilic substitution reaction. Haloalkanes are the substrate in these reactions. In this type of reaction, a nucleophile reacts with a haloalkane (the substrate) having a partial positive charge on the carbon atom bonded to the halogen. A substitution reaction takes place and the halogen atom, called the leaving group, departs as a halide ion. Since the substitution reaction is initiated by a nucleophile, it is called a nucleophilic substitution reaction.

It is one of the most useful classes of organic reactions of alkyl halides in which the halogen is bonded to an $sp^3$ hybridised carbon. The products formed by the reaction of haloalkanes with some common nucleophiles are given in Table 10.4.

Groups like cyanides and nitrites possess two nucleophilic centres and are called ambident nucleophiles. Actually, the cyanide group is a hybrid of two contributing structures and therefore can act as a nucleophile in two different ways $[^\ominus C \equiv N \leftrightarrow :C = N^\ominus]$, i.e., linking through carbon or nitrogen.
