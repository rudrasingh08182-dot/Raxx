**Example 2.1**
(a) Calculate the potential at a point $P$ due to a charge of $4 \times 10^{-7} \text{ C}$ located $9 \text{ cm}$ away.
(b) Hence obtain the work done in bringing a charge of $2 \times 10^{-9} \text{ C}$ from infinity to the point $P$. Does the answer depend on the path along which the charge is brought?

**Solution**
(a) $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r} = 9 \times 10^9 \times \frac{4 \times 10^{-7}}{0.09} = 4 \times 10^4 \text{ V}$
(b) $W = qV = 2 \times 10^{-9} \text{ C} \times 4 \times 10^4 \text{ V} = 8 \times 10^{-5} \text{ J}$

No, work done will be path independent. Any arbitrary infinitesimal path can be resolved into two perpendicular displacements: One along $r$ and another perpendicular to $r$. The work done corresponding to the latter will be zero.

### 2.4 POTENTIAL DUE TO AN ELECTRIC DIPOLE

As we learnt in the last chapter, an electric dipole consists of two charges $q$ and $-q$ separated by a (small) distance $2a$. Its total charge is zero. It is characterised by a dipole moment vector $\mathbf{p}$ whose magnitude is $q \times 2a$ and which points in the direction from $-q$ to $q$ (Fig. 2.5). We also saw that the electric field of a dipole at a point with position vector $\mathbf{r}$ depends not just on the magnitude $r$, but also on the angle between $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{p}$.
