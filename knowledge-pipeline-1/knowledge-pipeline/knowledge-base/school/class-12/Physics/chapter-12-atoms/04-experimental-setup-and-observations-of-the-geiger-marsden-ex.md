Figure 12.2 shows a schematic diagram of this experiment. Alpha-particles emitted by a $^{214}_{83}\text{Bi}$ radioactive source were collimated into a narrow beam by their passage through lead bricks. The beam was allowed to fall on a thin foil of gold of thickness $2.1 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}$. The scattered alpha-particles were observed through a rotatable detector consisting of a zinc sulphide screen and a microscope. The scattered alpha-particles on striking the screen produced brief light flashes or scintillations. These flashes may be viewed through a microscope and the distribution of the number of scattered particles may be studied as a function of angle of scattering.

A typical graph of the total number of $\alpha$-particles scattered at different angles, in a given interval of time, is shown in Fig. 12.3. The dots in this figure represent the data points and the solid curve is the theoretical prediction based on the assumption that the target atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus. Many of the $\alpha$-particles pass through the foil. It means that they do not suffer any collisions. Only about 0.14% of the incident $\alpha$-particles scatter by more than 1°; and about 1 in 8000 deflect by more than 90°.
