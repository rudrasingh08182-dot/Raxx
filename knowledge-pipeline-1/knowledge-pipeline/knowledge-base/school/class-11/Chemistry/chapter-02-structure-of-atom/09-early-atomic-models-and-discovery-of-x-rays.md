Different atomic models were proposed to explain the distributions of these charged particles in an atom. Although some of these models were not able to explain the stability of atoms, two of these models, one proposed by J.J. Thomson and the other proposed by Ernest Rutherford are discussed below.

### 2.2.1 Thomson Model of Atom
J. J. Thomson, in 1898, proposed that an atom possesses a spherical shape (radius approximately $10^{-10}$ m) in which the positive charge is uniformly distributed. The electrons are embedded into it in such a manner as to give the most stable electrostatic arrangement (Fig. 2.4). Many different names are given to this model, for example, plum pudding, raisin pudding or watermelon.

In the later half of the nineteenth century different kinds of rays were discovered, besides those mentioned earlier. Wilhelm Röntgen (1845-1923) in 1895 showed that when electrons strike a material in the cathode ray tubes, they produce rays which can cause fluorescence in the fluorescent materials placed outside the cathode ray tubes. Since Röntgen did not know the nature of the radiation, he named them X-rays and the name is still carried on.
