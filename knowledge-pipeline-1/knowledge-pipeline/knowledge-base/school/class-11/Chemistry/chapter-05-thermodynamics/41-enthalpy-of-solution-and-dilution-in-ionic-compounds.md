The enthalpy of solution of $AB(s)$, $\Delta_{sol}H^\circ$, in water is, therefore, determined by the selective values of the lattice enthalpy, $\Delta_{lattice}H^\circ$ and enthalpy of hydration of ions, $\Delta_{hyd}H^\circ$ as:

$\Delta_{sol}H^\circ = \Delta_{lattice}H^\circ + \Delta_{hyd}H^\circ$

For most of the ionic compounds, $\Delta_{sol}H^\circ$ is positive and the dissociation process is endothermic. Therefore the solubility of most salts in water increases with rise of temperature. If the lattice enthalpy is very high, the dissolution of the compound may not take place at all. Why do many fluorides tend to be less soluble than the corresponding chlorides? Estimates of the magnitudes of enthalpy changes may be made by using tables of bond energies (enthalpies) and lattice energies (enthalpies).

(f) Enthalpy of Dilution
It is known that enthalpy of solution is the enthalpy change associated with the addition of a specified amount of solute to the specified amount of solvent at a constant temperature and pressure. This argument can be applied to any solvent with slight modification. Enthalpy change for dissolving one mole of gaseous hydrogen chloride in 10 mol of water can be represented by the following equation. For convenience we will use the symbol aq. for water:

$HCl(g) + 10 \text{ aq.} \rightarrow HCl \cdot 10 \text{ aq. } \quad \Delta H = -69.01 \text{ kJ/mol}$

Let us consider the following set of enthalpy changes:

(S-1) $HCl(g) + 25 \text{ aq.} \rightarrow HCl \cdot 25 \text{ aq. } \quad \Delta H = -72.03 \text{ kJ/mol}$

(S-2) $HCl(g) + 40 \text{ aq.} \rightarrow HCl \cdot 40 \text{ aq. } \quad \Delta H = -72.79 \text{ kJ/mol}$

(S-3) $HCl(g) + \infty \text{ aq.} \rightarrow HCl \cdot \infty \text{ aq. } \quad \Delta H = -74.85 \text{ kJ/mol}$

The values of $\Delta H$ show general dependence.
