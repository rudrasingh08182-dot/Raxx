In fact, fluorine is so reactive that it attacks water and displaces the oxygen of water:

$2H_2O(l) + 2F_2(g) \rightarrow 4HF(aq) + O_2(g)$ (7.40)

It is for this reason that the displacement reactions of chlorine, bromine and iodine using fluorine are not generally carried out in aqueous solution. On the other hand, chlorine can displace bromide and iodide ions in an aqueous solution as shown below:

$Cl_2(g) + 2KBr(aq) \rightarrow 2KCl(aq) + Br_2(l)$ (7.41)
$Cl_2(g) + 2KI(aq) \rightarrow 2KCl(aq) + I_2(s)$ (7.42)

As $Br_2$ and $I_2$ are coloured and dissolve in $CCl_4$, they can easily be identified from the colour of the solution. The above reactions can be written in ionic form as:

$Cl_2(g) + 2Br^-(aq) \rightarrow 2Cl^-(aq) + Br_2(l)$ (7.41a)
$Cl_2(g) + 2I^-(aq) \rightarrow 2Cl^-(aq) + I_2(s)$ (7.42b)

Reactions (7.41) and (7.42) form the basis of identifying $Br^-$ and $I^-$ in the laboratory through the test popularly known as ‘Layer Test’. It may not be out of place to mention here that bromine likewise can displace iodide ion in solution:

$Br_2(l) + 2I^-(aq) \rightarrow 2Br^-(aq) + I_2(s)$ (7.43)

The halogen displacement reactions have a direct industrial application. The recovery of halogens from their halides requires an oxidation process, which is represented by:

$2X^- \rightarrow X_2 + 2e^-$ (7.44)

Here $X$ denotes a halogen element. Whereas chemical means are available to oxidise $Cl^-$, $Br^-$ and $I^-$, as fluorine is the strongest oxidising agent.
