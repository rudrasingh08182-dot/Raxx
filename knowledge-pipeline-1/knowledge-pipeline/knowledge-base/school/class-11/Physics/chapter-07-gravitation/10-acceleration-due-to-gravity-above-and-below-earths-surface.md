If the mass $m$ is situated on the surface of earth, then $r = R_E$ and the gravitational force on it is, from Eq. (7.10):
$F = \frac{GM_E m}{R_E^2}$ (7.11)

The acceleration experienced by the mass $m$, which is usually denoted by the symbol $g$ is related to $F$ by Newton’s 2nd law by relation $F = mg$. Thus:
$g = \frac{GM_E}{R_E^2}$ (7.12)

Acceleration $g$ is readily measurable. $R_E$ is a known quantity. The measurement of $G$ by Cavendish’s experiment (or otherwise), combined with knowledge of $g$ and $R_E$ enables one to estimate $M_E$ from Eq. (7.12). This is the reason why there is a popular statement regarding Cavendish: “Cavendish weighed the earth”.

7.6 ACCELERATION DUE TO GRAVITY BELOW AND ABOVE THE SURFACE OF EARTH

Consider a point mass $m$ at a height $h$ above the surface of the earth as shown in Fig. 7.8(a). The radius of the earth is denoted by $R_E$. Since this point is outside the earth, its distance from the centre of the earth is $(R_E + h)$. If $F(h)$ denoted the magnitude of the force on the point mass $m$, we get from Eq. (7.5):
$F(h) = \frac{GM_E m}{(R_E + h)^2}$ (7.13)

The acceleration experienced by the point mass is $g(h) = F(h)/m$ and we get:
$g(h) = \frac{GM_E}{(R_E + h)^2}$ (7.14)

This is clearly less than the value of $g$ on the surface of earth: $g = \frac{GM_E}{R_E^2}$. For $h << R_E$, we can expand the RHS of Eq. (7.14):
$g(h) = \frac{GM_E}{R_E^2 (1 + h/R_E)^2} = g(1 + h/R_E)^{-2}$

For $h/R_E << 1$, using binomial expression,
$g(h) \cong g(1 - 2h/R_E)$ (7.15)

Equation (7.15) thus tells us that for small heights $h$ above the value of $g$ decreases by a factor $(1 - 2h/R_E)$.
