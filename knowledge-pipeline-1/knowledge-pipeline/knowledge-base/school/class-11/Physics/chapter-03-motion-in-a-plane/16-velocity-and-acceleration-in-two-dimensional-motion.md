The direction of the average velocity $\vec{v}$ is shown in figures (a), (b) and (c) for three decreasing values of $\Delta t$, i.e. $\Delta t_1, \Delta t_2$, and $\Delta t_3$, ($\Delta t_1 > \Delta t_2 > \Delta t_3$). As $\Delta t \to 0$, $\Delta \vec{r} \to 0$ and is along the tangent to the path [Fig. 3.13(d)]. Therefore, the direction of velocity at any point on the path of an object is tangential to the path at that point and is in the direction of motion.

We can express $\vec{v}$ in a component form:

$\vec{v} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta \vec{r}}{\Delta t} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \left( \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \hat{i} + \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t} \hat{j} \right)$ (3.29)

$= \left( \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \right) \hat{i} + \left( \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t} \right) \hat{j}$

Or, $\vec{v} = v_x \hat{i} + v_y \hat{j} = \frac{dx}{dt} \hat{i} + \frac{dy}{dt} \hat{j}$

where $v_x = \frac{dx}{dt}, v_y = \frac{dy}{dt}$ (3.30a)

So, if the expressions for the coordinates $x$ and $y$ are known as functions of time, we can use these equations to find $v_x$ and $v_y$.

The magnitude of $\vec{v}$ is then

$v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$ (3.30b)

and the direction of $\vec{v}$ is given by the angle $\theta$:

$\tan \theta = \frac{v_y}{v_x}, \theta = \tan^{-1} \left( \frac{v_y}{v_x} \right)$ (3.30c)

$v_x, v_y$ and angle $\theta$ are shown in Fig. 3.14 for a velocity vector $\vec{v}$ at point P.
