To increase the length of a thin steel wire of $0.1 \text{ cm}^2$ cross-sectional area by $0.1\%$, a force of $2000 \text{ N}$ is required. The force required to produce the same strain in aluminium, brass and copper wires having the same cross-sectional area are $690 \text{ N}$, $900 \text{ N}$ and $1100 \text{ N}$ respectively. It means that steel is more elastic than copper, brass and aluminium. It is for this reason that steel is preferred in heavy-duty machines and in structural designs. Wood, bone, concrete and glass have rather small Young’s moduli.

**Example 8.1** A structural steel rod has a radius of $10 \text{ mm}$ and a length of $1.0 \text{ m}$. A $100 \text{ kN}$ force stretches it along its length. Calculate (a) stress, (b) elongation, and (c) strain on the rod. Young’s modulus of structural steel is $2.0 \times 10^{11} \text{ N m}^{-2}$.

**Answer**
We assume that the rod is held by a clamp at one end, and the force $F$ is applied at the other end, parallel to the length of the rod. Then the stress on the rod is given by:

$\text{Stress} = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{F}{\pi r^2} = \frac{100 \times 10^3 \text{ N}}{3.14 \times (10^{-2} \text{ m})^2} = 3.18 \times 10^8 \text{ N m}^{-2}$

The elongation, $\Delta L = \frac{(F/A)L}{Y} = \frac{(3.18 \times 10^8 \text{ N m}^{-2})(1 \text{ m})}{2.0 \times 10^{11} \text{ N m}^{-2}} = 1.59 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m} = 1.59 \text{ mm}$

The strain is given by:
$\text{Strain} = \frac{\Delta L}{L} = \frac{1.59 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}}{1 \text{ m}} = 1.59 \times 10^{-3} = 0.16 \%$

**Example 8.2** A copper wire of length $2.2 \text{ m}$ and a steel wire of length $1.6 \text{ m}$, both of diameter $3.0 \text{ mm}$, are connected end to end. When stretched by a load, the net elongation is found to be $0.70 \text{ mm}$. Obtain the load applied.

**Answer**
The copper and steel wires are under a tensile stress because they have the same tension (equal to the load $W$) and the same area of cross-section $A$. From Eq. (8.7) we have $\text{stress} = \text{strain} \times \text{Young’s modulus}$. Therefore:

$\frac{W}{A} = Y_c \times \left(\frac{\Delta L_c}{L_c}\right) = Y_s \times \left(\frac{\Delta L_s}{L_s}\right)$

where the subscripts $c$ and $s$ refer to copper and stainless steel respectively. Or,

$\frac{\Delta L_c}{\Delta L_s} = \left(\frac{Y_s}{Y_c}\right) \times \left(\frac{L_c}{L_s}\right)$

Given $L_c = 2.2 \text{ m}$, $L_s = 1.6 \text{ m}$. From Table 9.1, $Y_c = 1.1 \times 10^{11} \text{ N m}^{-2}$, and $Y_s = 2.0 \times 10^{11} \text{ N m}^{-2}$.

$\frac{\Delta L_c}{\Delta L_s} = \left(\frac{2.0 \times 10^{11}}{1.1 \times 10^{11}}\right) \times \left(\frac{2.2}{1.6}\right) = 2.5$
